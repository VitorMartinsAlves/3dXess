RULES GUIDE

Chess rules, made clear.

Learn how the pieces move, what check means, and which situations bring a game to an end.

Objective and setup

Chess is played by two people on a 64-square board. White moves first. The goal is to checkmate the opposing king: attack it so that no legal response can prevent its capture.

How the pieces move

Pawn

Moves one square forward; on its first move it may advance two. It captures one square diagonally and cannot move backward.

Rook

Moves any number of squares horizontally or vertically, without jumping over pieces.

Knight

Moves in an L shape: two squares in one direction and one perpendicular. It is the only piece that can jump over others.

Bishop

Moves any number of squares diagonally, without jumping over pieces.

Queen

Combines the movement of the rook and bishop.

King

Moves one square in any direction, provided it does not move to an attacked square.

Special moves

Castling

A joint move by the king and a rook. It is allowed only if neither has moved, the path is clear, and the king is not in check or crossing an attacked square.

En passant

A special pawn capture that is available only immediately after the opponent advances a pawn two squares and leaves it beside yours.

Promotion

When a pawn reaches the last rank, it must promote to a queen, rook, bishop, or knight.

Check and draws

Check

The king is under attack. The player must get out of check with a legal move.

Checkmate

The king is in check and there is no legal response. The game ends.

Stalemate

The player is not in check but has no legal move. The game is drawn.

Repetition

A position repeated three times may be claimed as a draw; five repetitions make the draw automatic.

Move-count rule

Fifty moves by each side without a pawn move or capture allow a draw claim; seventy-five make the draw automatic, unless the last move was checkmate.

This summary covers standard chess rules. Tournament-specific situations may depend on the competition regulations.
